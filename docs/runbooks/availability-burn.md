# Runbook: availability

Two families of alert route here, and the first thing to establish is which one woke you.

**Budget alerts** — `StorefrontAvailabilitySLOBurn`, `CatalogAvailabilitySLOBurn`,
`RecsAvailabilitySLOBurn`. Generated by Sloth. Read the `service` and `severity` labels;
both severities share one `alertname`.

**Symptom alerts** — `ServiceHardDown`, `ServiceReceivingNoTraffic`, `DependencyHardDown`,
`SLOBudgetNearlyExhausted`. Hand-written in
`gitops/slo/handwritten/symptom-rules.yaml` and labelled `alert_kind: symptom`. They exist
because burn-rate alerting is deliberately slow and short total outages slip under it. Jump
to [Symptom alerts](#symptom-alerts) for those.

## What a budget alert means

A share of the service's 30-day error budget has been spent faster than the budget can
afford. This is not "some requests failed". Requests fail continuously and that is what the
budget is for. The alert says the spending rate is high enough that continuing at this rate
exhausts the month.

| Service | SLO | Budget | 5xx ratio that pages at 14.4x |
|---|---|---|---|
| `storefront` | 99.5% / 30d | 3h 36m | 7.2% |
| `catalog` | 99.9% / 30d | 43m 12s | 1.44% |
| `recs` | 99.0% / 30d | 7h 12m | 14.4% |

The numerator counts `status_code=~"5.."` only. 404 and 409 are not in it, and probe and
scrape traffic is not in the denominator (`docs/slo-methodology.md`).

## Page or ticket

**`severity: page`** — 14.4x over 1h+5m, or 6x over 6h+30m. 2% or 5% of the month's budget
is already gone. Something is broken now. Work it now.

**`severity: ticket`** — 3x over 24h+2h, or 1x over 72h+6h. About 10% of the budget, spent
slowly. Nobody should be woken for it. File it and look during working hours; the usual
cause is a chronic small error rate, not an incident, and it will still be there in the
morning.

Both windows in a tier must be true at once, so a budget alert fires when the *long* window
crosses, at roughly `60 min × threshold / e` for an observed error ratio `e`:

| Service | Observed 5xx | Page fires after |
|---|---|---|
| `storefront` | 100% | 4m 19s |
| `storefront` | 50% | 8m 38s |
| `storefront` | 10% | 43m 12s |
| `catalog` | 100% | 51.8s |
| `recs` | 100% | 8m 38s |

Two things follow. If you have just been paged, the fault has been running for minutes, not
seconds — start by asking what changed ten minutes ago, not ten seconds ago. And an outage
shorter than the row above pages nobody, which is the whole reason the symptom alerts exist.

## Symptom alerts

These fire on what is happening rather than on what it costs. They carry
`alert_kind: symptom` and evaluate every 15s rather than the global 30s.

| Alert | Condition | Delay before it fires | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ServiceHardDown` | serving traffic, 100% of it 5xx | ~3m (`[2m]` window + `for: 1m`) | page |
| `ServiceReceivingNoTraffic` | request rate flat zero | ~8m (`[5m]` window + `for: 3m`) | page |
| `DependencyHardDown` | every call to one dependency failing | ~3m (`[2m]` window + `for: 1m`) | page |
| `SLOBudgetNearlyExhausted` | under 10% of a 30-day budget left | 15m | ticket |

**`ServiceHardDown`** means total failure of a service that is still answering. Go straight
to the failure modes below; the budget alerts for the same service will follow in minutes if
it continues, and their absence is not reassurance.

**`ServiceReceivingNoTraffic`** is the one people misread. It is not "quiet, probably fine".
Every ratio SLI is `0/0` in this state, so the burn-rate alerts are not merely silent —
they are structurally incapable of firing, and the SLO dashboards are lying by omission
until it is fixed. Two usual causes: the pods went NotReady and were removed from their
Service (check `kubectl -n demo get endpoints`), or the load generator stopped
(`kubectl -n demo get deploy trafficgen`).

**`DependencyHardDown`** names the cause rather than the symptom. `storefront` returning
503s and `catalog` being unable to reach Postgres are the same incident; this alert says
which end to look at. Read the `service` and `dependency` labels — `storefront/catalog`
points at catalog or its database, `catalog/postgres` points at Postgres directly.

Note that during a Postgres outage you will generally see `storefront/catalog` and **not**
`catalog/postgres`: catalog goes NotReady, stops serving, and therefore stops making
instrumented database calls, so its side of the metric goes quiet rather than going to
failure. The outer edge of the failure is the one that stays visible.

**`SLOBudgetNearlyExhausted`** is not an incident. It is a level check on what is left, and
it catches the case a rate-based rule cannot: a service sitting just under every burn-rate
threshold for days still arrives at zero budget. The action is to stop shipping risk to that
service until the window rolls over, not to investigate an outage.

## First five minutes

Start the port-forwards if they are not up:

```bash
bash scripts/urls.sh
```

**Is this a chaos run?** Check before anything else. If it is, someone is measuring and
touching the cluster will invalidate the run.

```promql
sum by (service, kind) (rate(fault_injected_total[5m]))
```

Non-zero means faults are being injected deliberately through `PUT /internal/faults` or the
canary path. Also check `ls chaos/harness/out/` and whether a `run.py` process is alive.

**Which service is actually failing, and on what route?**

```promql
sum by (service, status_code) (rate(http_requests_total[5m]))

sum by (service, route, status_code) (rate(http_requests_total{status_code=~"5.."}[5m]))
```

**Where does the burn stand?**

```promql
slo:current_burn_rate:ratio
slo:period_error_budget_remaining:ratio
```

`current_burn_rate` above 1 means the budget is being spent faster than it regenerates;
above 14.4 is page territory. `period_error_budget_remaining` is a ratio of the 30-day
allowance — but see the limitations in `docs/slo-methodology.md` before quoting it, because
on a cluster this short-lived it means "since Prometheus started".

**Is anything not serving?**

```bash
kubectl -n demo get pods -o wide
kubectl -n demo get endpoints storefront catalog recs
kubectl -n demo get rollout storefront
kubectl get nodes
```

Note that **a pod serving 100% injected 500s still reports Ready** (ADR 0007). Pod status
does not tell you whether a service is healthy in this platform. The SLI does.

**Dashboards.** Grafana at `http://localhost:3000` (`admin` / `demo-not-a-secret`).
"SLO overview — error budgets" (uid `slo-overview`) for burn rates and remaining budget
across all six SLOs; `storefront — service SLO` and its catalog/recs siblings (uids
`slo-storefront`, `slo-catalog`, `slo-recs`) for RED metrics; "Platform overview — cluster
health" for cluster context.

## Telling this system's failure modes apart

### recs degrading (expected; should not page storefront)

`recs` returns 5xx or times out. storefront catches it in `asyncio.gather` and serves the
product page with `recommendations: []` and `recommendations_degraded: true`.

- `RecsAvailabilitySLOBurn` fires. `StorefrontAvailabilitySLOBurn` does **not**.
- storefront logs `serving without recommendations` at WARNING, with `product_id` and
  `error`.
- Only 15% of load-generator traffic hits `GET /products/{id}` at all, so even a total recs
  outage touches 15% of storefront requests and degrades rather than fails them.

```promql
sum by (service) (rate(http_requests_total{status_code=~"5.."}[5m]))
sum by (service, dependency, outcome) (rate(dependency_requests_total{service="storefront"}[5m]))
```

If only `recs` is burning, the graceful degradation is working. This is a ticket, not an
incident.

### catalog failing hard (storefront burns too)

storefront cannot degrade around a missing product. `_upstream_error` turns a catalog
failure into a 503, so both SLIs burn together.

- Both `CatalogAvailabilitySLOBurn` and `StorefrontAvailabilitySLOBurn`, catalog first
  (its budget is 5x tighter, so its threshold is crossed at a fifth of the error rate).
- `dependency_requests_total{service="storefront", dependency="catalog", outcome!="ok"}`
  climbing.
- storefront logs `upstream failure` with `dependency: catalog`.

### Postgres gone (the single replica)

The one to check early, because it looks different from everything else.

```bash
kubectl -n demo get pods -l app.kubernetes.io/name=postgres
kubectl -n demo get endpoints catalog

# catalog's readiness payload names the dependency directly.
# (The image is python:3.12-slim with no curl or wget, so forward the port instead of exec.)
kubectl -n demo port-forward deploy/catalog 18000:8000 >/dev/null 2>&1 &
curl -s localhost:18000/readyz; echo
kill %1
```

catalog's `/readyz` pings the asyncpg pool, so when Postgres is gone **every catalog replica
leaves the Service within about ten seconds**. `/readyz` returns `{"database": "down"}`.

The distinctive signature is an absence, not a spike. catalog's own `http_requests_total`
denominator collapses toward zero, so `CatalogAvailabilitySLOBurn` shows **no data** rather
than a burn — a `0/0` SLI cannot cross any threshold. A catalog SLI that has gone quiet is
not a healthy catalog. The alerts that should fire here are `DependencyHardDown` on
`storefront/catalog` and, if it lasts, `ServiceReceivingNoTraffic` on catalog. storefront's
503s do the actual budget burning.

Even that is not guaranteed to page. `DependencyHardDown` needs about three minutes of
continuous total failure, and a Postgres pod reschedule on kind can finish inside a minute.
An outage that ends before anything fires is a correct outcome for the alerting policy and a
genuinely interesting line for an incident report — say so rather than filing it as a gap.

Recovery is bounded by pod scheduling plus volume attach — on EKS an EBS detach and attach,
several minutes — and there is nothing to fail over to (ADR 0005). Wait for it.

### A canary in progress

```bash
kubectl argo rollouts get rollout storefront -n demo
```

Split the SLI by revision. If the errors are confined to one hash, it is the canary:

```promql
sum by (rollouts_pod_template_hash) (rate(http_requests_total{service="storefront", status_code=~"5.."}[2m]))
/
sum by (rollouts_pod_template_hash) (rate(http_requests_total{service="storefront"}[2m]))
```

At 25% weight, a canary failing half its requests reads as roughly 12% across the Service —
enough to page storefront while three-quarters of the fleet is perfectly healthy. The
analysis should be aborting on its own within about two minutes of the first canary pod
serving (`initialDelay: 60s`, `interval: 30s`, `failureLimit: 2`). If it is not, see
"Rolling back".

### A node drain in progress

```bash
kubectl get nodes                       # look for SchedulingDisabled
kubectl -n demo get pods -o wide        # are pods moving?
```

A drain should produce a latency bump and no 5xx: `maxUnavailable: 1` in each PDB,
`maxUnavailable: 0` on the rollouts, and a `preStop: sleep 5` on every service container to
let endpoint removal win its race against SIGTERM. If a drain is producing 5xx, the preStop
pause or the PDB is the thing to look at, not the application.

### Everything is burning at once

Check whether the platform is measuring itself wrongly before assuming the platform is
broken:

```promql
up{namespace="demo"}
sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="storefront"}[1m]))
```

A storefront request rate near zero with the load generator running means the failure is in
ingress-nginx or the trafficgen pod, not in the services. `kubectl -n demo get deploy
trafficgen` and `kubectl -n ingress-nginx get pods`.

## Rolling back

Roll back **only** when the errors are attributable to a specific revision — a canary in
progress, or a rollout promoted in the last few minutes. Rolling back does nothing for a
dependency outage and costs a fleet-wide restart.

```bash
# Stop the bleeding: return all traffic to the stable ReplicaSet.
kubectl argo rollouts abort storefront -n demo

# Roll the spec back to the previous revision.
kubectl argo rollouts undo storefront -n demo
```

`abort` changes Rollout status, so Argo CD leaves it alone. **`undo` changes the pod
template, and the storefront Application has `selfHeal: true` — Argo CD will put the bad
template back within seconds.** The durable fix is a revert in Git; `undo` buys time until
that lands.

An aborted Rollout stays `Degraded` until a new template arrives, so the Argo CD Application
shows unhealthy afterwards. `make rollout-retry` (`kubectl argo rollouts retry rollout
storefront -n demo`) restarts it once the fix is in Git;
`kubectl argo rollouts promote storefront -n demo --full` clears it back to a known-good
template.

For a non-storefront service, the equivalent is `kubectl -n demo rollout undo
deployment/catalog` with the same `selfHeal` caveat — revert the image tag in
`gitops/services/catalog/overlays/<env>/kustomization.yaml` and let Argo CD deliver it.

## What not to do

**Do not restart pods to "fix" a dependency outage.** Liveness is deliberately tolerant
(`periodSeconds: 10`, `failureThreshold: 6` — about a minute) precisely so Kubernetes does
not do this on your behalf. Restarting catalog while Postgres is down parks the new pod in
its `wait-for-postgres` init container, removing it from the Service for *longer* than
leaving it alone; and it opens a fresh asyncpg pool on the other side. It also destroys the
in-process fault config, which silently ends any chaos run in progress mid-measurement.

**Do not scale up to absorb errors.** The offered load is a fixed 8 req/s arrival rate
(k6's `constant-arrival-rate`), not a queue. More replicas do not change an error *ratio*
caused by a failing dependency, and they add cold pools and more probe traffic.

**Do not edit the PrometheusRule or the Rollout in the cluster.** `slo-rules` and the
service Applications all run `selfHeal: true`; the edit is reverted within seconds and the
only thing it achieves is one more revision in the audit trail. If an alert genuinely needs
quieting, use an Alertmanager silence with an expiry. If a manifest needs changing, change
it in Git.

**Do not call it recovered when the fault stops.** The SLIs use 5-minute rate windows, so
the signal trails the system by several minutes. The harness measures recovery from when
`slo:current_burn_rate:ratio` returns below 1.0 and *stays* there for 180 seconds
(`settle_s`), and so should you.

**Do not delete the Chaos Mesh resource and walk away.** If an experiment is mid-run,
`chaos/harness/run.py` removes its own faults in a `finally` block. Killing the harness
process is the safe way to stop; deleting objects underneath it leaves the run recorded as
a false result.

## After

If this was a real incident rather than a scenario, write it up with
`evidence/templates/incident-report.md` and add it to `evidence/`. The template matches what
the harness generates, so hand-written and measured incidents sit in the same table.
